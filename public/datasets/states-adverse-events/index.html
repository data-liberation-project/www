These requests were filed by MuckRock journalists Michael Nolan and Dillon Bergin.

The Data Liberation Project filed requests to over 30 states so far to find out which have reporting requirements for adverse events and data that is responsive to public records requests.

The requests described the data as “All adverse medical event and sentinel event records” reported to the state health department.

Adverse events are a core part of patient safety. They’re medical errors and their consequences, an idea that captured the field of health care’s attention after the Institute of Medicine’s 1999 report that laid the problem of patient harm bare. The report sparked decades of nationwide efforts, but a recent Office of Inspector General report still found that “patient harm events in hospitals remain a serious concern.”

In 2005, the Patient Safety and Quality Improvement Act was signed into law and created a new system for health care organizations to report adverse events through the Agency for Health Care Research and Quality. AHRQ also created “common formats” that aim to standardize types of adverse events so that reporting across the country uses similar terms and definitions. Federal rules and policies like these outline how providers can report events that eventually make it into federal patient safety databases. The key is that this federal reporting is voluntary and can leave gaps in the types and amount of harm reported. A 2025 OIG report found that, similar to a study the office conducted in 2012, hospitals reported few patient harm events to Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services or their state.

OIG surveyed state requirements for reporting adverse events at the time of the study in 2018 and found that 26 states and the District of Columbia had reporting requirements. The type of requirements and reporting are different in each state. OIG found that requirements often focus on reporting the most serious types of harm and not all events. Some states, like Pennsylvania or California, have more robust reporting requirements. Pennsylvania, for example, created an independent state agency called the Patient Safety Authority, and describes itself as “the only state that requires health care facilities to report all instances of harm (i.e., serious events) or potential for harm (i.e., incidents).”
